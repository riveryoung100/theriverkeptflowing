There is a kind of exhaustion that comes from doing difficult work. There is another kind that comes from doing work I was never asked to carry. From the outside, the two can look almost identical. Both can fill a calendar. Both can produce something useful. Both can earn the approval of people who see the result without seeing what the work is costing.

I am learning to ask a question that productivity alone cannot answer: Is this actually mine to do?

That question unsettles me because I can usually find a good reason to keep going. Someone needs help. An opportunity could disappear. I have the ability. The result might matter. None of those reasons is necessarily false. But a good reason to care about something is not always a calling to become responsible for all of it.

## When activity crowds out attention

In [Luke 10:38–42](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke+10%3A38-42&version=WEB), Martha welcomes Jesus and becomes distracted by the serving, while Mary sits and listens to him. Martha asks Jesus to intervene. His response draws attention to her anxiety and to the good Mary has chosen.

I do not read that as permission to neglect the work of caring for people. Someone still has to prepare meals, keep promises, and carry ordinary responsibilities. What challenges me is the possibility of becoming so occupied with serving that I stop listening to the One I intend to serve.

I can be doing something for God while becoming inattentive to God. I can use activity to avoid the quieter work of prayer, surrender, and honest examination. Being busy gives me something visible to point toward. Listening asks me to admit that I may need to change direction.

## Ability is only part of discernment

Being capable makes this difficult. Once I know I can do something, refusing it can feel like wasting a gift. If people are used to depending on me, stepping back can feel like letting them down.

But ability does not tell me everything about an assignment. It does not tell me whether this is the right season, whether I have already made a more important commitment, or whether another person needs room to carry a responsibility of their own.

Sometimes what looks like generosity is partly fear. I am afraid of disappointing someone. Afraid of losing an opportunity. Afraid that if I stop holding everything together, something will happen that I cannot control. The work may be good, but fear is deciding how much of it I take on.

That deserves honesty. I cannot discern faithfully while calling every anxious yes obedience.

## Begin with what has already been entrusted

The people closest to me should not receive only what remains after every possible opportunity has been served. My family, my attention, and the promises I have already made belong in the discernment too.

I want to ask whether an additional responsibility will make me more faithful to those commitments or increasingly unavailable to them. I want to notice when the work meant to serve a life begins consuming that life. And I want to distinguish doing my part from trying to guarantee an outcome that has never been mine to control.

This does not make every decision easy. Some assignments are demanding. Some seasons require sacrifice. Weariness alone does not prove that I should leave. But the amount of effort a task requires cannot prove that God assigned it either.

## Leave room to listen

I am trying to bring a simpler prayer into the work: Help me recognize what You have given me to do. Help me release what I have taken on out of fear. Help me carry real responsibilities with love, and help me stop confusing control with faithfulness.

Sometimes the answer may be to continue. Sometimes it may be to finish a promise carefully and decline the next one. Sometimes it may be to ask for help or let someone else lead.

I do not want a life that is impressive because I managed to carry everything. I want a life whose work reflects the One I am following. That requires more than effort. It requires enough room to hear him.
