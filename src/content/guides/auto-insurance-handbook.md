      <section id="welcome" class="handbook-introduction"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="eyebrow">Welcome to River Guides</p><h2>Auto insurance should make sense before you ever need it.</h2>
        <p class="lead">Most people do not wake up excited to learn about auto insurance.</p>
        <p>They buy it because the law requires it, a lender requires it, or they need proof of coverage to stay on the road. Then they compare prices, choose a policy, and hope they never have to think about it again.</p>
        <p>The problem is that auto insurance is one of the largest financial contracts many people agree to without understanding what they purchased.</p>
        <div class="question-stack" aria-label="Common auto insurance questions"><p>What does “full coverage” actually include?</p><p>How much liability protection do I have?</p><p>What happens if an uninsured driver hits me?</p><p>Could I afford my deductible tomorrow?</p></div>
        <p>This handbook is not designed to pressure you into buying every available coverage. It is designed to help you understand the choices, compare policies honestly, and select protection that fits your own life.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section class="part-divider" id="part-1"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading"><p class="part-number">Part I</p><h2>Before You Buy</h2><blockquote>Good insurance is not about buying the most coverage or the least coverage. It is about buying the right protection for your situation.</blockquote></div></section>

      <section id="chapter-1" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 1</p><h2>Insurance Is Not Just a Price</h2>
        <p>Most shoppers begin with one question: “How much is it?”</p>
        <p>Price matters. Insurance competes with housing, groceries, utilities, childcare, vehicle payments, and every other part of a household budget. But price cannot be evaluated honestly until the protection is understood.</p>
        <p>Two people may both say they have “full coverage” while carrying completely different liability limits, deductibles, uninsured motorist protection, rental reimbursement, and roadside assistance.</p>
        <p>A lower premium may represent a better rate. It may also represent less protection. The only way to know is to compare the policies line by line.</p>
        <aside class="takeaway-card"><p class="card-label">River Guide takeaway</p><p>Compare protection first. Compare price second.</p></aside>
        <h3>What an insurance agent should actually do</h3>
        <p>A good agent does more than generate a number. A good agent asks about your household, vehicles, assets, budget, health coverage, loans, risk tolerance, and existing protections before making recommendations.</p>
        <p>Insurance is personal. Two families living next door to each other may need different policies because their finances, vehicles, drivers, loans, and ability to absorb a loss are different.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-2" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 2</p><h2>Understanding Risk</h2>
        <p>Before discussing liability limits, deductibles, collision, or comprehensive, begin with one question:</p><div class="pull-quote">What are you trying to protect?</div>
        <p>Your vehicle is only part of the answer. Depending on your circumstances, your auto policy may also help protect your savings, income, future earnings, assets, transportation, and your family's financial stability.</p>
        <p>Some customers genuinely need the lowest lawful price so they can remain insured and stay on the road. Others own property, have significant savings, or want broader limits because they have more to protect.</p>
        <p>Neither priority is automatically wrong. The important thing is understanding the tradeoffs before making the choice.</p>
        <div class="part-summary"><p class="card-label">Key lessons from Part I</p><ul><li>“Full coverage” is not a standardized policy definition.</li><li>Price matters, but it must be compared alongside protection.</li><li>The right policy depends on budget, assets, vehicles, and risk tolerance.</li><li>A good agent educates before recommending.</li></ul></div>
      </div></section>

      <section class="part-divider" id="part-2"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading"><p class="part-number">Part II</p><h2>Understanding Core Coverage</h2><blockquote>Every line on a policy exists to address a specific financial risk.</blockquote></div></section>

      <section id="chapter-3" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 3</p><h2>Liability Coverage</h2>
        <p>Liability coverage helps protect you financially when you are legally responsible for injuring someone or damaging another person's property, subject to your policy terms and limits.</p>
        <h3>Bodily Injury Liability</h3><p>Bodily Injury Liability may help pay covered costs arising from injuries you cause to other people, including medical treatment, lost income, legal defense, and other damages when applicable.</p>
        <h3>Property Damage Liability</h3><p>Property Damage Liability may help pay for damage you cause to another person's vehicle or property, such as a fence, building, utility pole, sign, mailbox, or guardrail.</p>
        <h3>Understanding split limits</h3><p>Limits are often written as three numbers, such as <strong>50/100/50</strong>. This generally means:</p>
        <ul class="clean-list"><li>$50,000 maximum for bodily injury to one person</li><li>$100,000 maximum for bodily injury to everyone in one accident</li><li>$50,000 maximum for property damage in one accident</li></ul>
        <p>These are not deductibles. They are maximum policy limits for covered liability claims.</p>
        <h3>Choosing limits</h3><p>Liability recommendations should consider assets, income, vehicle values, risk tolerance, and what a person could realistically lose after a serious at-fault accident.</p>
        <p>State minimum coverage may satisfy the law, but it may leave substantial personal exposure. For many drivers without major assets, limits around 50/100 may offer a practical balance between affordability and protection. Drivers with more to protect may consider higher limits, such as 100/300, after discussing cost and suitability with a licensed professional.</p>
        <aside class="notice-card"><h3>State requirements vary</h3><p>Required limits, available policy forms, and liability rules differ by state. Confirm the requirements and options where you live.</p></aside>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-4" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 4</p><h2>Collision Coverage</h2>
        <p>Collision coverage may help repair or replace your vehicle after it collides with another vehicle or object, or rolls over, after your deductible and subject to the policy.</p>
        <ul class="clean-list"><li>Another vehicle</li><li>A tree or guardrail</li><li>A building or fence</li><li>A ditch</li><li>Another object in the roadway</li></ul>
        <p>Collision is coverage on your own policy for your own vehicle. If another driver is legally responsible, their liability coverage may ultimately pay, or your insurer may seek recovery after handling your covered claim.</p>
        <h3>Check your loan or lease</h3><p>A lender or leasing company may require collision coverage and may limit how high your deductible can be. A deductible that lowers your premium still must satisfy your financing agreement.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-5" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 5</p><h2>Comprehensive Coverage</h2>
        <p>Comprehensive coverage generally addresses covered physical damage to your vehicle that is not caused by a collision.</p>
        <ul class="clean-list"><li>Theft</li><li>Fire</li><li>Hail or windstorm</li><li>Flood or falling objects</li><li>Vandalism</li><li>Glass damage, depending on the policy</li><li>Contact with an animal</li></ul>
        <p>“Comprehensive” does not mean everything is covered. Every policy includes definitions, exclusions, conditions, and limits.</p>
        <div class="part-summary"><p class="card-label">Key lessons from Part II</p><ul><li>Liability protects against covered harm you cause to others.</li><li>Collision generally addresses impact and rollover damage to your vehicle.</li><li>Comprehensive generally addresses covered non-collision damage.</li><li>Loans and leases may require physical-damage coverage and deductible limits.</li></ul></div>
      </div></section>

      <section class="part-divider" id="part-3"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading"><p class="part-number">Part III</p><h2>Building the Right Policy</h2><blockquote>The risk you decline does not disappear. It becomes a risk you agree to carry yourself.</blockquote></div></section>

      <section id="chapter-6" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 6</p><h2>Uninsured &amp; Underinsured Motorist Coverage</h2>
        <p>This coverage is designed to help protect you from drivers who have no insurance or not enough insurance, subject to state law and your policy.</p>
        <h3>A lesson I learned personally</h3><p>I once owed approximately $24,000 on a vehicle when I was hit by an uninsured driver who did not have a license. I did not carry uninsured motorist property damage coverage.</p>
        <p>I was left with a damaged vehicle, an outstanding loan, and the need to find another way to keep moving. Eventually, I stopped paying the loan and bought another vehicle with cash. It became a costly financial mess that could have looked very different over what had been only a small premium difference.</p>
        <div class="pull-quote">I did not fully appreciate uninsured motorist property damage until I became the customer.</div>
        <h3>Uninsured Motorist Bodily Injury</h3><p>This coverage may help with covered injury-related losses when an uninsured or, where applicable, underinsured driver causes an accident.</p>
        <h3>Uninsured Motorist Property Damage</h3><p>Where available, this coverage may help pay for covered damage to your vehicle caused by an uninsured driver.</p>
        <p>Many people believe “full coverage” automatically includes uninsured motorist protection. It may not. Review the actual policy rather than relying on the label.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-7" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 7</p><h2>Medical Payments and Personal Injury Protection</h2>
        <p>Depending on the state and insurer, an auto policy may offer Medical Payments Coverage, Personal Injury Protection, or both.</p>
        <p>These coverages may help with covered medical or injury-related expenses after an accident. PIP may also address certain additional losses, depending on state law and policy terms.</p>
        <p>Existing health insurance, deductibles, out-of-pocket costs, household needs, and state requirements may affect the decision. The goal is to understand how the coverages work together rather than assume one automatically replaces another.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-8" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 8</p><h2>Optional Coverages That Can Make a Big Difference</h2>
        <h3>Rental Reimbursement</h3><p>Rental reimbursement may help pay for temporary transportation while your vehicle is being repaired after a covered loss, subject to daily and total limits.</p>
        <p>One of the most common post-accident problems is not the repair itself. It is figuring out how to get to work, school, appointments, and family responsibilities while the vehicle is unavailable.</p>
        <h3>Roadside Assistance</h3><p>Depending on the policy, roadside assistance may help with towing, a dead battery, a flat tire, a lockout, or emergency fuel delivery.</p>
        <h3>GAP Protection</h3><p>GAP coverage is generally intended to address some or all of the difference between a covered total-loss settlement and the remaining loan balance, subject to its terms. Some drivers already have it through a lender or dealership.</p>
        <h3>Ride-share use</h3><p>Tell your agent if you drive for a ride-share or delivery platform. A personal auto policy may exclude or limit coverage during commercial activity, and a ride-share endorsement or other coverage may be required. The platform may also require proof of coverage.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-9" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 9</p><h2>Choosing Deductibles Wisely</h2>
        <p>The deductible is the amount you agree to pay toward a covered physical-damage claim before the insurer pays the remaining covered amount.</p>
        <p>Many customers choose either $500 or $1,000. Do not assume the higher deductible creates meaningful savings. Ask for both options every time.</p>
        <p>Sometimes doubling the deductible changes the premium by only a few dollars each month. For higher-risk profiles, the difference may be larger. The only responsible answer comes from comparing the actual numbers.</p>
        <p>The right deductible is not merely the one with the lowest premium. It is one you could realistically afford tomorrow without creating another emergency.</p>
        <aside class="takeaway-card"><p class="card-label">River Guide takeaway</p><p>Never assume. Compare $500 and $1,000 deductibles across the entire policy.</p></aside>
        <div class="part-summary"><p class="card-label">Key lessons from Part III</p><ul><li>Uninsured motorist protection can address losses caused by someone else's lack of coverage.</li><li>Medical coverages should be evaluated alongside existing protections and state rules.</li><li>Rental, roadside, GAP, and ride-share coverage address distinct risks.</li><li>A deductible must fit both the premium and your emergency fund.</li></ul></div>
      </div></section>

      <section class="part-divider" id="part-4"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading"><p class="part-number">Part IV</p><h2>Buying Auto Insurance With Confidence</h2><blockquote>A good insurance purchase happens when you understand exactly what you are buying.</blockquote></div></section>

      <section id="chapter-10" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 10</p><h2>Comparing Quotes the Right Way</h2>
        <p>The goal is not simply to find a lower monthly payment. The goal is to determine whether one policy provides equal or better protection at a better price.</p>
        <ul class="clean-list"><li>Bodily injury and property damage limits</li><li>Collision and comprehensive deductibles</li><li>Uninsured and underinsured motorist protection</li><li>Medical Payments or PIP</li><li>Rental reimbursement</li><li>Roadside assistance</li><li>Drivers, vehicles, and endorsements</li></ul>
        <p>If the comparison is not apples to apples, the lower premium does not tell you which policy provides better value.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-11" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 11</p><h2>Reading Your Declarations Page</h2>
        <p>Your declarations page is the summary of the policy. It generally identifies the named insureds, covered drivers and vehicles, policy period, limits, deductibles, optional coverages, discounts, and premiums.</p>
        <p>When comparing insurance, provide the current declarations page whenever possible. It allows a new proposal to be measured against what you actually have instead of what anyone remembers or assumes.</p>
        <aside class="takeaway-card"><p class="card-label">River Guide takeaway</p><p>Your declarations page is the starting point for a responsible comparison.</p></aside>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-12" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 12</p><h2>Discounts That Can Save You Money</h2>
        <p>Bundling homeowners and auto insurance can produce a meaningful multi-policy discount with many insurers.</p>
        <p>Depending on the company, landlord policies, secondary residences, renters, condominium coverage, boats, recreational vehicles, or off-road vehicles may also create or support discounts. The effect is not always reciprocal: adding a boat or off-road vehicle may receive a discount without further reducing the home or auto premium.</p>
        <ul class="clean-list"><li>Multi-vehicle discounts</li><li>Pay-in-full discounts</li><li>Automatic-payment discounts</li><li>Paperless billing</li><li>Other company-specific programs</li></ul>
        <p>Discount structures vary. Ask how each added policy affects the total household cost.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-13" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 13</p><h2>From Quote to Active Policy</h2>
        <p>Before binding, confirm every driver, vehicle, registered owner, address, financing company, loan or lease, prior carrier, prior policy number, and required state filing.</p>
        <h3>SR-22 and FR-44 filings</h3><p>Some states require certain drivers to file proof of financial responsibility, often after specified violations, uninsured driving, or a license suspension. The state generally tells the driver when a filing is required.</p>
        <h3>Household drivers</h3><p>Disclose household members and regular vehicle users accurately. A driver who does not live in the household often needs a separate policy, though insurer and state rules vary. Do not add someone solely for convenience without asking the carrier how the risk must be written.</p>
        <h3>Lenders and leasing companies</h3><p>A financing or leasing company generally needs to be listed appropriately because it has a financial interest in the vehicle.</p>
        <h3>Choosing the effective date</h3><p>Always ask exactly when the new policy begins. Do not cancel the old policy until the new policy is active.</p>
        <div class="pull-quote">The old policy should generally end on the same date the new policy begins.</div>
        <p>Cancelling too early can create a lapse. Cancelling too late can create overlapping premiums. When properly notified, the prior insurer may adjust the cancellation to the requested date and refund unearned premium according to its rules.</p>
        <aside class="notice-card"><h3>Cancellation terms vary</h3><p>Personal auto policies are often cancellable before expiration, but fees, short-rate calculations, notice requirements, and outstanding earned premium can vary by state, insurer, and policy. Confirm the terms rather than assuming there can never be a charge.</p></aside>
        <h3>Binding and proof of insurance</h3><p>After the information, coverages, payment, and effective date are confirmed, the policy can be bound. Save the insurance ID cards to your phone and keep them accessible whenever you drive.</p>
        <div class="part-summary"><p class="card-label">Key lessons from Part IV</p><ul><li>Use the current declarations page for a true comparison.</li><li>Bundle discounts can improve value, but structures vary by insurer.</li><li>Confirm drivers, owners, lenders, and filings before binding.</li><li>Coordinate effective and cancellation dates to avoid a lapse or unnecessary overlap.</li></ul></div>
      </div></section>

      <section class="part-divider" id="part-5"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading"><p class="part-number">Part V</p><h2>Living With Your Policy</h2><blockquote>Buying the policy is not the end of the relationship. It is the beginning of the protection.</blockquote></div></section>

      <section id="chapter-14" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 14</p><h2>After You Buy Your Policy</h2>
        <p>Auto insurance is not “set it and forget it.” Tell your agent when you move, add or remove a vehicle, add or remove a driver, change vehicle use, refinance, lease, pay off a loan, or begin ride-share activity.</p>
        <h3>Endorsements and policy changes</h3><p>Policy changes are commonly completed through endorsements. Your agent or service team can help with additions, removals, coverage changes, lienholder updates, and cancellations.</p>
        <h3>After paying off a vehicle</h3><p>Paying off a vehicle is a good time to review physical-damage coverage. For an older vehicle with a lower actual cash value, some drivers consider removing collision and comprehensive while retaining liability and, where available and appropriate, uninsured motorist protection.</p>
        <p>The decision should consider the vehicle's value, premium savings, risk tolerance, and whether you could replace the vehicle yourself.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-15" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 15</p><h2>Claims</h2>
        <p>Claims processes vary, but reporting usually involves describing what happened and providing relevant information such as photographs, the location, other parties' information, witnesses, and a police report when available.</p>
        <p>Sales and claims are different functions. An agent helps prepare protection before the loss. Claims professionals investigate and adjust the loss under the policy after it occurs.</p>
        <p>Some of the most difficult claim-related problems concern transportation: “How will I get to work?” and “Who pays for the rental?” That is why rental reimbursement should be considered before the vehicle enters a repair shop.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-16" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 16</p><h2>Renewal Time</h2>
        <p>Every renewal is an opportunity to review—not necessarily to switch, but to confirm the policy still fits.</p>
        <ul class="clean-list"><li>Have my drivers, vehicles, or address changed?</li><li>Have I paid off a loan?</li><li>Do my liability limits still fit what I have to protect?</li><li>Could I afford my deductibles today?</li><li>Am I missing rental, roadside, or uninsured motorist coverage?</li><li>Am I receiving all available discounts?</li></ul>
        <p>Long tenure with one company does not guarantee the lowest rate. Loyalty can be meaningful, but it should not prevent a responsible comparison when price or needs change.</p>
        <p>When a quote is too expensive, it is reasonable to decline and revisit the market near the next renewal. A professional relationship can continue even when the current quote is not a win.</p>
      </div></section>

      <section id="chapter-17" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 17</p><h2>Working With a Great Insurance Agent</h2>
        <p>A great agent does not merely quote. A great agent asks questions, explains each line, identifies exposures, compares deductibles, confirms existing protections, and customizes the proposal.</p>
        <p>Customers often want the final price immediately. A responsible producer slows the conversation down enough to gather accurate information and explain the coverages before presenting the number.</p>
        <h3>Follow-up should have a next appointment</h3><p>“Call me if you decide” allows good conversations to drift into limbo. A better process is to schedule the next conversation before ending the current one, so both people know when the comparison will continue.</p>
        <aside class="takeaway-card"><p class="card-label">River Guide takeaway</p><p>Great agents customize. Average agents quote.</p></aside>
        <div class="part-summary"><p class="card-label">Key lessons from Part V</p><ul><li>Update the policy when life, drivers, vehicles, or vehicle use changes.</li><li>Review physical-damage coverage after paying off an older vehicle.</li><li>Claims reveal the value of choices made before the accident.</li><li>A strong agent educates, customizes, and follows up deliberately.</li></ul></div>
      </div></section>

      <section class="part-divider" id="part-6"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading"><p class="part-number">Part VI</p><h2>Final Principles Every Driver Should Remember</h2><blockquote>Insurance is not about expecting the worst. It is about making sure one bad day does not become a lifetime financial setback.</blockquote></div></section>

      <section id="chapter-18" class="chapter"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="chapter-number">Chapter 18</p><h2>The Principles I Want Every Driver to Remember</h2>
        <ol class="principles-list">
          <li><h3>Do not shop on price alone.</h3><p>A small monthly saving can create a much larger uncovered loss.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Always compare apples to apples.</h3><p>Match limits, deductibles, drivers, vehicles, and optional coverages first.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Understand a coverage before declining it.</h3><p>Ask what risk becomes yours if the coverage is removed.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Review your insurance when life changes.</h3><p>Vehicles, homes, loans, drivers, addresses, and work use all matter.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Cancel the previous policy correctly.</h3><p>Verify the new effective date, then coordinate the old cancellation date.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Keep your agent informed.</h3><p>The insurer can only evaluate the risk it knows about.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Be careful adding drivers.</h3><p>Household and non-household drivers may need different treatment.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Disclose ride-share or delivery use.</h3><p>Commercial activity may change what the personal policy covers.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Ask questions until the policy makes sense.</h3><p>A good insurance professional should welcome careful questions.</p></li>
          <li><h3>Choose the policy that fits your life.</h3><p>The best policy is the one you understand and can responsibly maintain.</p></li>
        </ol>
      </div></section>

      <section id="final-word" class="final-reflection"><div class="handbook-shell handbook-shell--reading">
        <p class="eyebrow">A final word</p><h2>Understanding is part of protection.</h2>
        <p>When I first started helping people with auto insurance, I thought my job was to find the best price.</p>
        <p>Over time, I realized the more important work was helping people understand the decisions they were making.</p>
        <p>Every customer has a different story. Some are buying their first vehicle. Some are raising teenagers. Some are retiring. Some are protecting very little today. Others have spent decades building everything they own.</p>
        <p>There is no single perfect auto policy. There is only the policy that fits the person, the family, the finances, the vehicles, and the risks.</p>
        <p>Drive safely. Protect what you have worked hard to build. Keep asking good questions.</p><p class="signature">— River Young</p>
      </div></section>
